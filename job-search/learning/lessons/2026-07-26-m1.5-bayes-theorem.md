## Concept

Bayes' theorem answers one question: **given new evidence, how should I update what I believed before?** That's it. Everything else is bookkeeping.

```
                P(Evidence | Hypothesis) · P(Hypothesis)
P(Hypothesis | Evidence) = ──────────────────────────────────────
                              P(Evidence)

        posterior          =         likelihood  ×  prior
                                    ─────────────────────────
                                          evidence
```

Four pieces, plain English:
- **Prior** `P(H)` — what you believed *before* seeing this piece of evidence (e.g. "1% of transactions are fraud," based on history).
- **Likelihood** `P(E|H)` — if the hypothesis were true, how likely is this evidence? (e.g. "if it's fraud, how often does the model flag it?")
- **Evidence** `P(E)` — how likely is this evidence *overall*, across every hypothesis (fraud and not-fraud combined). This is the normalizer that makes the posterior a real probability.
- **Posterior** `P(H|E)` — your **updated** belief after seeing the evidence. It becomes tomorrow's prior if more evidence arrives — Bayesian updating is naturally sequential.

The one-line intuition to keep forever: **the posterior is a blend of what you already knew (prior) and what the new data tells you (likelihood), and the blend depends on how strong/plentiful each one is.** Strong prior + weak/rare evidence → posterior stays close to the prior. Weak prior + lots of evidence → posterior is dominated by the data.

**Bayesian vs frequentist — what's actually different:**

| | Frequentist | Bayesian |
|---|---|---|
| Treats the **parameter** (e.g. true conversion rate) as | Fixed, unknown constant | A random variable with a distribution |
| Treats the **data** as | Random (imagine repeating the experiment infinitely) | Fixed — it's the one dataset you observed |
| "Probability" means | Long-run frequency over repeated trials | Degree of belief, updatable with evidence |
| Output | p-value, confidence interval ("95% of such intervals would contain the true value") | Posterior distribution ("there's an 87% probability the true rate is above X") |
| Prior info | Not used (by design) | Explicitly incorporated as a prior |

Neither is "more correct" — they're different philosophies that often converge with enough data. Frequentist methods dominate where strict, auditable error control matters (clinical trials, regulated finance). Bayesian methods shine when you have **little data but real prior knowledge** to lean on, need continuous/sequential decisions, or want an answer that's directly interpretable as "the probability that X is true" rather than a confusing p-value statement.

## Worked example

**The classic base-rate problem — but framed the way you'll actually meet it: fraud detection**, close cousin of the anomaly-detection work you did at VCDI (P11).

> A fraud-detection model catches **99% of actual fraud** (sensitivity/recall) and **incorrectly flags 1% of genuine transactions** (false positive rate). Only **1% of all transactions are actually fraudulent** (the prior/base rate). A transaction gets flagged. What's the probability it's *actually* fraud?

Gut instinct says "99%." Let's do the arithmetic with **natural frequencies** — the trick that makes Bayes' theorem stop feeling like magic. Imagine 10,000 transactions:

```
                        10,000 transactions
                               │
            ┌──────────────────┴──────────────────┐
       Fraud: 1% prior                      Genuine: 99% prior
         = 100 txns                            = 9,900 txns
            │                                       │
   ┌────────┴────────┐                   ┌──────────┴──────────┐
 99% TPR           1% miss            1% FPR                99% correct
   │                  │                  │                     │
FLAGGED          not flagged          FLAGGED              not flagged
  99                  1                 99                   9,801
  (TP)               (FN)              (FP)                  (TN)

Total FLAGGED = 99 (true fraud) + 99 (false alarms) = 198
```

```
P(Fraud | Flagged) = TP / (TP + FP) = 99 / 198 = 50%
```

Via the formula, same answer:
```
P(Fraud|Flag) = P(Flag|Fraud)·P(Fraud) / P(Flag)
              = (0.99 × 0.01) / (0.99×0.01 + 0.01×0.99)
              = 0.0099 / 0.0198
              = 0.50
```

**A "99% accurate" model is right about fraud only 50% of the time it flags something.** Why? Because fraud is *rare* — genuine transactions vastly outnumber fraudulent ones, so even a tiny 1% false-positive rate applied to 9,900 genuine transactions generates as many false alarms (99) as the true catches (99). The base rate dominates.

Notice something: `TP / (TP+FP)` is exactly **precision** — a metric you already know cold from the propensity-model and threshold work you've been doing. **Precision *is* a Bayesian posterior**: it's literally `P(actually positive | predicted positive)`. Every time you've reported precision, you've been doing Bayes' theorem without naming it.

## Interview angle

- **"A test is 99% accurate, the condition is rare — what's P(condition | positive)?"** is one of the most common DS screening questions (disease testing, fraud, spam, churn). The trap is **base-rate neglect**: confusing `P(evidence|hypothesis)` with `P(hypothesis|evidence)` — assuming the test's accuracy *is* the answer, ignoring how rare the thing is. Always draw the natural-frequency tree (10,000 people/transactions) — it's the fastest way to get the arithmetic right live in an interview and to *show* the interviewer you understand why, not just that you memorised a formula.
- **Class imbalance link (straight from P6):** this is *why* a highly accurate model can still have poor precision on a rare category — the same reason you did class-weighting, hyperparameter tuning and the Pareto manual-review split on the ~A$12B spend classifier. If asked "why isn't accuracy enough for imbalanced classes," Bayes' theorem is the rigorous *why*.
- **Naive Bayes classifier:** a classic text-classification baseline (TF-IDF/bag-of-words + Bayes' theorem + a "naive" assumption that features are conditionally independent given the class). You used logistic regression for P6, but if asked "why not Naive Bayes for spend categorisation," a good answer: Naive Bayes is fast and a strong baseline, but the independence assumption is often badly violated by correlated text features, and logistic regression directly optimises the decision boundary rather than a generative class model — usually wins when you have enough data and engineered features (as you did: spend value, line count).
- **Bayesian priors ↔ regularisation ↔ cold-start shrinkage (ties directly to what you just studied for propensity models):** L2/ridge regularisation is mathematically the **MAP (maximum a posteriori) estimate** of a Bayesian linear model with a Gaussian prior centred at zero on the coefficients — the "penalty" is just a prior pulling estimates toward zero when data is thin. Same idea drives **cold-start shrinkage**: when a customer/item has too little data, you don't trust their raw sample rate — you blend it with a population-level prior, weighted by how much data that customer has (this is **empirical Bayes / James-Stein shrinkage**). Small n → posterior stays close to the population prior; large n → posterior is dominated by that customer's own likelihood. If you're asked "how would you handle a customer with only 3 purchases in a propensity model," this is the crisp Bayesian answer.
- **Bayesian vs frequentist A/B testing** — a real, current debate (see Trend below): frequentist gives a p-value/CI that's often misread; Bayesian gives a direct "probability variant B is better," and supports peeking/early stopping without inflating false-positive rate the way naive frequentist peeking does. The trap: interviewers sometimes push "so Bayesian is strictly better" — the honest answer is it trades transparent, audit-friendly error control (why regulated/high-stakes testing still leans frequentist) for a more intuitive, data-efficient answer that depends on a defensible prior.

## Trend of the day

The Bayesian-vs-frequentist debate isn't academic — it's playing out in industry experimentation platforms right now. Bayesian A/B testing can shorten experiments materially (industry estimates suggest 20-50% faster decisions vs fixed-sample frequentist tests) because it supports continuous "probability of uplift" monitoring rather than a single end-of-test verdict — genuinely useful when traffic is scarce. But frequentist methods remain the backbone at Google, Microsoft and Amazon-scale experimentation programs, and are still the standard in regulated fields (clinical trials, finance) precisely because of their strict, auditable error-rate guarantees. The realistic industry position — and a good interview answer — is "know both, and justify which one fits the stakes and data volume of the decision," not "Bayesian has won."

## Self-check
1. A spam filter has 98% recall on spam and a 2% false-positive rate on genuine email. Only 0.5% of all email is spam. Out of 10,000 emails, roughly how many will be flagged as spam, and what fraction of those flagged are *actually* spam?
2. In plain English, what's the difference between what a frequentist confidence interval says and what a Bayesian credible interval says?
3. A new customer has made only 2 purchases and both were of Product A. Naively you'd predict P(buys A again) is very high. Why would a Bayesian approach likely give a more moderate estimate, and what's the general name for this technique?

## Answers
1. Spam: 0.5% of 10,000 = 50 emails; 98% caught = 49 flagged (TP), 1 missed. Genuine: 9,950 emails; 2% false-positive rate = ~199 flagged (FP). Total flagged ≈ 49 + 199 = 248. P(actually spam | flagged) = 49/248 ≈ **20%** — even a "98% accurate" filter is wrong about 4 times out of 5 when it flags something, because spam is rare. Same base-rate mechanism as the fraud example.
2. A **frequentist CI** says: "if I repeated this exact sampling process many times, 95% of the intervals I'd construct would contain the true fixed parameter" — it's a statement about the *procedure*, not a probability about this specific interval. A **Bayesian credible interval** says directly: "given my prior and this data, there's a 95% probability the true parameter lies in this interval" — a direct probability statement about the parameter itself, which is usually what people intuitively (if wrongly) think a frequentist CI means.
3. With only 2 data points, the sample is far too small and noisy to trust on its own — a Bayesian approach combines that thin likelihood with a **prior** (e.g. the population-average repurchase rate across all customers), so the posterior estimate shrinks back toward the population average rather than swinging to an extreme based on 2 observations. This is **Bayesian/empirical-Bayes shrinkage** — exactly the fix for the cold-start problem (too little data on one entity) in propensity/recommendation models.
